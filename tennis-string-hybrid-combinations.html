Hybrid string combinations, worked out

Most hybrid advice stops at "poly mains, soft crosses". These are 115 specific pairings drawn from strings already rated here, generated separately for each playing style — topspin, flat hitting, power, precision, arm-first, all-round and string-breaking — so the list is not one long ranking of spin.

Open the string bench →

Read this first: these are calculated, not tested. Every row below is a combination of two strings whose individual ratings are on this site. The combination has not been strung and hit. The model is simple and stated in full in the next section so you can disagree with it. Treat these as a shortlist worth trying, not as measurements — the 148 named hybrids on the hybrids page are the rated ones.

The model, in full

The mains stretch further, move more and do the snapback, so they carry most of the spin, control, precision and durability. The crosses settle the bed and absorb part of the impact, so they carry most of the feel, comfort and sound. Power sits between the two. Each axis is a weighted blend of the two components on that same axis — mains-weighted 0.75 for spin, 0.70 for control and longevity, but only 0.38 for feel and 0.40 for arm friendliness.

What the model does not know: how a particular pair actually behaves together. Cross strings saw through mains at different rates, some combinations move and stay moved, and tension interacts with both materials. Those are real effects and none of them are in these numbers.

One shortlist per playing style

An earlier version of this page ranked everything by spin, which quietly assumed every reader hits topspin. Seven styles now get their own pass, each with its own entry test and its own ranking: topspin-heavy, flat hitter and control, power and big serve, precision and touch, arm-first, all-round, and string breakers. A pairing has to earn its place on the axes that style actually cares about, and it has to beat its own mains at something — otherwise there was no reason to string two strings.

Both directions are generated. Firm mains with a softer cross is the common one; soft mains with a firmer cross — gut or multifilament in the mains — is the setup a good part of the professional tour plays, and an earlier cut of this list excluded it by accident.

What makes a pair eligible is simply that the two strings differ, not a threshold either one has to clear on its own. That matters more than it sounds: an earlier version required a mains string to rate 6 or better for longevity, which quietly excluded Babolat RPM Blast, Pro Hurricane Tour, Luxilon ALU Power Soft, Savage and Yonex Poly Tour Drive from every combination on this page. Durability was never a requirement for a mains string — it is a trade-off some of the most popular polyesters deliberately lose. Every string on the bench can now take part.

A later version required the cross to be at least two points softer than the mains. Tested against the 33 hybrids curated here by hand, that rule admitted one in five — because it rejected every poly-on-poly pairing, two firm strings put together for durability and control rather than comfort, which is ordinary stringing-room practice. All three directions are generated now: softer cross, firmer cross, and level.

Out of 39,362 pairings considered, each style keeps at most 18, each construction archetype is topped up to at least six so no pattern is missing, no main appears more than twice, no cross more than three times, and no two rows share an identical eight-axis profile. Some beds serve two styles, which is why the total is 115 rather than 126.

Browse by construction

A hybrid is defined as much by what the two strings are made of as by which strings they are. These are the six patterns, each with the beds here that follow it. The material of each component is read from its name where the name states it, and otherwise inferred from its ratings — nothing is claimed about a construction the data does not show.

Gut mains, poly cross — the tour pattern (6)

Natural gut in the mains for feel and power, a shaped polyester in the crosses to hold the ball down and stop the gut sawing itself apart. This is the setup a good part of the professional tour plays, and the reason is not mysterious: gut does everything except discipline a big swing, and the poly cross supplies exactly that. It is also the most expensive bed here and among the shortest-lived.

Poly on poly — the durability hybrid (19)

Two polyesters, usually a shaped one in the mains for bite and a smoother or softer one across. Nobody strings this for comfort; it is strung for control, for tension stability, and because a smooth cross lets the mains snap back instead of locking in place.

Before you string one

Ask your stringer about the tension offset: when the crosses are the softer string they are usually pulled a little lower so the material is not stretched into stiffness. And expect a hybrid to wear differently from a full bed — the mains usually notch first, and a soft cross against a shaped poly can saw through faster than either string manages alone.
