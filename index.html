Racket department: 155 numbered frames — the full current lines of Head, Wilson, Yonex, Dunlop, Diadem, Tecnifibre, Babolat, Prince, Solinco, Lacoste and Pro Kennex — rated across eleven axes with a difficulty column, an amber search bar that filters the bench or looks specs up online, and a bronze transfer-specs form that scores a researched frame from its specs. Log a customization

Tell the bench what you did — lead tape, grip build-up, a different butt cap. Give it the new numbers and the frame is re-scored from them.
what you can actually do — and what it moves
Lead at 10 & 2 — swingweight up roughly a point per gram: plow-through, power and stability climb; maneuverability drops and difficulty creeps up.
Lead at 12 (the tip) — the strongest swingweight gain per gram: depth and stability rise most, fast exchanges get hardest.
Lead at 3 & 9 — twist resistance: off-center hits calm down and feel steadies, with only a small swingweight rise.
Silicone or lead in the handle — mass rises but balance pulls toward the hand: feel, comfort and stability climb while the frame stays quick.
Overgrip build-up — a few grams at the very bottom: headlight, whippier through contact, the scores barely move.
The estimator reads three numbers — weight, swingweight, balance — but you can simply write what you did in words: “6 g lead at 12”, “silicone in the handle”. The panel reads the grams and the placement, works the numbers out with the standard mass-placement math, fills the fields above, and the preview re-scores instantly. Type your own numbers any time — they always win over the derived ones.

frame ⇆ string calibrator v1
matchup department
Which string belongs in which frame. Scored from the physics of the pairing — stiffness compounding, snapback spin, power budget, pattern wear — using each frame's filed specs and each bed's lab data. Your preference sliders don't touch this table: this is what the equipment asks for, not what you like.
The model, and where it comes from. Four findings from published racquet science drive every score here. 1 · Shock compounds. Frame stiffness (RA) and string-bed dynamic stiffness add up into one system; impact-shock work at Tennis Warehouse University and in Rod Cross & Crawford Lindsey's Technical Tennis / The Physics and Technology of Tennis shows a stiff frame strung with a stiff poly transfers the most shock to the arm — so the calibrator rewards soft-in-stiff and flexible-frame-with-poly pairings and flags stiff-on-stiff. 2 · Spin is snapback. High-speed TWU studies traced most spin to the mains sliding sideways and snapping back; slick co-polys do this best, and a dense 18×20 pattern — which pinches that movement — gains the most from them, while open patterns add bite to almost anything. 3 · The power budget is shared. A launchy frame plus a lively bed overshoots; a muted frame plus a board of a bed plays dead. The calibrator scores the sum of frame power and string power against a middle band, which is why control polys top the list in powerful frames and livelier beds float up in low-powered ones. 4 · Patterns eat strings. Open patterns let strings move more, so thin or heavily shaped beds wear fastest exactly where they bite hardest — the longevity penalty you'll see on some pairings. The classic exceptions are priced in too: natural-gut hybrids as the historic arm-saver in stiff frames, poly mains for spin control in everything modern. Tensions are read as filed per bed; in a stiff-on-stiff system, dropping 1–2 kg buys back comfort before any string change does.
